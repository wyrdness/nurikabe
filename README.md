## Overview

Nurikabe (塗壁, ぬりかべ, 'plastered wall') is a yōkai documented in folk traditions of northern Kyushu — particularly Onga County in former Chikuzen Province (modern Fukuoka Prefecture) and adjacent parts of Ōita Prefecture. It manifests as an invisible obstruction that suddenly bars a traveler's path on a road at night: attempts to walk around or above it fail, since the wall extends as needed. Folklorist Kunio Yanagita's 1938 collection records that 'when one is walking a path at night, suddenly the destination one is going towards turns into a wall'. The traditional remedy, recorded in the same regional sources, is to sweep a stick along the bottom of the wall — striking the top has no effect. Until 2007 the creature was thought to lack any pre-modern visual representation; that year, a c. 1660 yōkai handscroll held by Brigham Young University was identified as containing a labelled illustration of nurikabe shown as a three-eyed, dog-elephant-like beast, which is now considered the earliest extant depiction. The modern, beloved visual identity of nurikabe — a great rectangular wall with arms, legs and a face — was created by manga artist Mizuki Shigeru, who said the design grew out of a personal experience of an invisible obstruction during the Pacific War in New Guinea. Through Mizuki's GeGeGe no Kitarō series the character became a national icon. Skeptics and some cultural commentators interpret the original folk experience as a sleep-deprivation, fog or ground-hugging mist phenomenon known to nighttime travelers in the Kyushu countryside.

- **Origin**: Japanese: 塗 (nuru, 'to plaster, paint, smear') + 壁 (kabe, 'wall')
- **Meaning**: 'Plastered wall' or 'painted wall'; also rendered 塗り壁
- **First use**: Recorded in 20th-century Kyushu folklore collections (notably Yanagita Kunio, 1938); the BYU c. 1660 scroll labels its creature 'nurikabe', placing the term in the mid-Edo period

## Theories & Explanations

### Paranormal / Cryptozoological

**Genuine yōkai obstruction** _(Northern Kyushu folk tradition)_

Traditional view that nurikabe is a discrete supernatural entity that impedes travelers as a manifestation of the night road's spiritual hazards.

### Scientific

**Sensory disorientation in fatigued travelers** _(Modern folkloric and skeptical commentators)_

Reports correspond to nighttime disorientation, fatigue, low-visibility fog and ground mist on rural Kyushu roads, in which lone walkers lose their bearing and feel unable to proceed.

### Skeptical

**Cultural construction by modern media** _(Contemporary yōkai scholars)_

While the folkloric kernel is genuine, the dominant mental image of nurikabe is a 20th-century creation by Mizuki Shigeru rather than an inherited visual tradition.
